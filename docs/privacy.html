Privacy

Ambrogio runs on a small computer you own, in your house. There is no account to create and no dashboard in someone else's data centre. This page says exactly what leaves your house, and why — including the parts that do.

The short version

There are two ways to run Ambrogio, and they are not the same for your privacy.

With a box of your own — the Raspberry Pi you flashed — your data lives on it, and the developer of Ambrogio receives nothing: no telemetry, no crash reports, no analytics, no copy of your messages. There is nowhere for us to look.

With the iPhone app on its own, which is how the current beta works if you have no box, there is no box to hold anything — so your questions travel to a server the developer runs, and from there to Anthropic. That server sees what you send it. It is named below, with what it keeps.

Everything that leaves is listed further down, by name. Nothing is left out because it was inconvenient.

Where your data actually is

If you have a box. Everything Ambrogio knows — your messages, your email, your calendar, what it remembers about your household — is stored on the Raspberry Pi (or equivalent computer) that you flashed and plugged in. At home the app talks to that box. When you are away it reaches the box over your own private network (Tailscale); the traffic is encrypted end to end between your phone and your box. Deleting your data means deleting it from your box, or unplugging the box. Nobody has to approve that, and no copy survives somewhere else.

If you don't. The app keeps your conversation and your entries on the phone itself, and there is no box in the picture. To answer you it has to reach a model, and unless you have supplied your own Anthropic key it does that through ai-gateway.iotexlab.io, a server the developer of Ambrogio runs. Deleting the app deletes what is on the phone; what that server holds is covered below.

There is also a case in between: a phone paired to a box, but out of reach of it (the “away” mode). Your entries stay on the box, but the questions you ask while away go through the same gateway.

What leaves, where it goes, and why

This is the whole list. Some of it depends on which shape you run and which features you use; where that matters, it says so.

  • The app → Ambrogio's gateway → Anthropic — when you have no box

    This is how the iPhone beta works unless you supply your own Anthropic key. What you type, and any photo or document you show it, goes to ai-gateway.iotexlab.io — a server run by the developer of Ambrogio — and from there to Anthropic. So does whatever Ambrogio looks up in order to answer: a meal you logged, a note, an event from your calendar. If you have connected Apple Health, asking about your diet carries the figures derived from it along the same path, because the answer is computed from them.

    That server keeps your conversation so it can carry it on from one message to the next, and it keeps it as a file on its disk, not only in memory: one transcript per conversation, holding what you wrote and what came back. It is kept for one reason, and the reason has a limit: after ten minutes of silence the server lets your conversation's process go, and the file is what lets the next message continue it instead of re-sending and re-paying for everything said so far. There is no version of this that keeps the continuity without the file — the toolkit underneath is explicit that a session which is not written down cannot be resumed.

    So it is kept, and not forever: a conversation is deleted seven days after its last message, together with the record that would have let the server pick it up again. And since a restart makes the older ones impossible to continue in any case, those are cleared out when the server next starts — a few minutes after, not the same instant, because a file still being written to might belong to a process that has not finished shutting down. It also records, per phone: a token it issued you, how many tokens of model capacity you have used today, and when it last saw you — so that one person cannot use up the shared allowance. The app never sends your name, your email or any Apple identifier — the label it registers with is the phone model, the language, and a code the app invents for itself. One exception, and it is worth knowing: if you were invited personally rather than with a shared code, the label whoever invited you typed is stored on your row, and that is often a first name. It answers on the developer's own Claude subscription, which is why there is an allowance at all.

    If you would rather this server were not in the path, there are two ways out, both supported: put your own Anthropic API key in the app, and it talks to Anthropic directly; or set up a box, and it talks to your box. Both take this server out of the path for answering you; the public lookups in the next item are made by the app itself and are unaffected by either. To have the token this server issued you revoked, or your row deleted, ask.

  • Your box → Anthropic (Claude) — when you have one

    Ambrogio thinks with Claude, using your own Claude subscription or API key, which you provide during setup. When you talk to Ambrogio, the box sends the conversation to Anthropic's API to get an answer, along with whatever context that answer needs — which can include the content of an email you asked about, a file you sent, or notes it has saved for you. This is the relationship between you and Anthropic, under your own credential and their terms; the developer of Ambrogio is not a party to it and never sees the traffic.

    One exception worth knowing. If your phone is paired to a box but cannot reach it — the “away” mode — the phone answers by itself, and those questions go through the gateway above, not through your box.

  • Public sources Ambrogio looks things up in

    When you ask for something it has to fetch, the question — or the part of it that is the query — goes to the service that answers it. These carry what you asked, not who you are: there is no account and no identifier attached.

    Weather: Open-Meteo (and its geocoding service, which receives the place name you typed), and api.met.no. Food: Open Food Facts and the USDA food database, which receive what you searched for. News: Google News, GNews and Hacker News. Markets: CoinGecko, Binance and Finnhub, which receive the symbol you asked about. The weather and food lookups happen from the app itself when you have no box; the rest run on the box.

  • Your own accounts, only the ones you connect

    Email: your box logs in to your mail provider over IMAP and sends over SMTP, with credentials you gave it. Calendar: if you connect iCloud, it speaks CalDAV to Apple's servers. Telegram: if you set it up, those messages travel through Telegram's servers like any other Telegram chat and their privacy policy applies. All three are optional, and none of them is on until you set it up.

  • Your box → Expo → Apple, for push notifications

    If you turn on notifications in the app, the box asks Expo's push service (exp.host) to ring your phone through Apple. The notification carries no content — only a generic category such as "New message", "Heads up" or "Urgent". The real text stays on your box and the app fetches it when you open it.

  • Tailscale, if you set up access from outside your home

    Reaching your box from outside uses Tailscale, which is a third-party company: you log in to them, and their coordination service knows your devices exist and helps them find each other. The traffic between your phone and your box is encrypted end to end and Tailscale cannot read it, but they are a party to the arrangement and this page would be wrong not to name them.

  • Analytics, telemetry, crash reports → nobody

    Never, in either shape. The app contains no analytics SDK, no crash reporter and no tracking of any kind; there is no advertising identifier, no profiling and no third-party measurement. Your box does not phone home, and updates are something you fetch from GitHub when you choose to. The gateway described above is not an exception: it sees the questions it is asked to answer, because it has to in order to answer them, and it is not used to build a profile of you or given to anyone else.

Apple Health

If you choose to connect Apple Health, Ambrogio reads these specific things, and nothing else:

  • Active energy burned and resting energy burned, per day — so the diet feature can show what you ate against what you spent.
  • Steps, per day — read in the same request as the two energies, and shown on the activity card.
  • Body mass and height — so you don't have to type them.

That is the whole list: five figures, and Ambrogio asks iOS for exactly those. It does not read your biological sex, your date of birth, your workouts, your heart rate, your sleep or your clinical records.

Ambrogio never writes anything to Apple Health. This is off until you turn it on, iOS asks you separately before any of it is readable, and you can withdraw it at any time in Settings → Health → Data Access & Devices. These figures are never used for advertising, marketing or profiling, and are never sold or shared with anyone. They are not put in iCloud by Ambrogio — though, like any app's data, whatever sits on the phone is included in an iPhone backup if you have iCloud Backup switched on, which is Apple's setting and not ours. Where they are kept depends on which shape you are running: with a box, on the box, alongside the rest of your data; without one, on the phone, and nowhere else until you ask about them.

One thing to be aware of

Ambrogio's answers are produced by Claude, so asking about your diet sends these figures along with the question — not only asking about the numbers themselves. The daily target is computed from them, so any answer about what you ate carries them. Without a box they pass through the gateway named above on the way to Anthropic.

Nothing is sent on Ambrogio's own initiative: the arithmetic happens where the figures are. If you would rather they never left, use the diet page and don't ask about it in chat.

What the app keeps on your phone

In the iOS keychain: the address of whatever answers you, and the credential for it — the token that pairs your phone to your box, or the token the gateway issued you, or the Anthropic key you supplied. Alongside it: your own display preferences, and a cached copy of the recent conversation so the app opens instantly.

Without a box, the phone is also where your own entries live — what you logged in the diet, your projects, the documents you kept — because there is nowhere else for them to be. Deleting the app deletes that. Keychain entries are the one thing that can outlive a delete on iOS, depending on the version and on whether the phone was restored from a backup, so if you want to be certain, clear the key in the app before removing it.

“Unpair this device” removes the token from your box. The gateway is less tidy than that, and the honest thing is to lay it out rather than use one word for all of it:

  • Revoking your token stops it working immediately. It leaves everything else: the row itself (its id, the phone-model label, the dates) and the running count of what it used.
  • Deleting the row removes that record.
  • A short usage trace outlives both: how much your token spent today, and when it was last seen. Deleting your row now takes that with it.
  • The transcripts described above are not filed under your name — the server has no index from a person to their conversations, and building one would mean knowing more about you, not less. They go on their own clock: seven days after a conversation's last message, or sooner when the server restarts.
  • Alongside each one the server keeps a fingerprint used to recognise a conversation it has seen before. That part is hashes, not text. Stored beside it are the instructions the app sends with every question — the app's own preamble and your language setting, not anything you typed. Both go on the same seven days.

Ask, and your token is revoked and your row and its usage trace removed — by hand, there is no self-service button. Conversations are the one thing that cannot be picked out for one person, so those go by the clock above. Saying otherwise would be the easiest lie on this page, and if any of it changes, it changes here first.

What the app asks your phone for

Besides Apple Health, the app asks iOS for three things, each only when you first use the feature that needs it, and each refusable:

  • Calendar — so Ambrogio can answer about your day. It reads events from every calendar account on the phone, because that is how iOS exposes them: iCloud, Google, Exchange, any CalDAV account you added in Settings. An event only leaves the phone when it is part of an answer you asked for.
  • Microphone and speech recognition — for the hold-to-talk button. Where the words are made depends on your phone and on a setting in the app: on-device recognition when it is available, otherwise the audio goes to Apple's speech service, the same one the system keyboard's dictation uses. The app tells you which of the two is happening.
  • Camera and photo library — only when you attach a photo or scan a document, and only for the file you pick.

Everyone in the household

An Ambrogio box can serve several people, each with their own data and their own permissions. Whoever set the box up administers it and can see that other members exist, what they are allowed to do, and anything explicitly shared with them — they cannot read another member's private conversations or mail. If a child uses Ambrogio, the same applies, and the adult who set the box up is responsible for what it is allowed to do.

Changes

If what leaves your house ever changes, this page changes with it and the date below moves. The image you flash and the flashing tool are published in this repository; the application source is not open at the moment, so most of what this page describes you cannot yet check for yourself. That is a reason to hold these statements to a higher standard, not a lower one — if something here turns out to be wrong, say so and it gets corrected.
